Accent Listening — Ear Training (for the teacher)

Why ear training matters

The IELTS Listening test plays recordings spoken in a mix of native accents — British, Australian, and American. The student hears each recording only once and must understand it well enough to answer. So the goal of this resource is purely receptive: the student must understand spoken English. The student is never asked to pronounce or speak anything here — they listen.

This is also one of the most practical skills for real life. Our student is preparing to live and work in an English-speaking environment. Following a manager's instructions, understanding a coworker, catching announcements, using the phone — all of this depends on a trained ear far more than on perfect grammar. Ear training is the bridge between "knows some English on paper" and "can function in an English-speaking workplace."

A Portuguese-speaking beginner has a specific challenge: Portuguese and English map sounds very differently (vowel length, the English "th", reduced/unstressed syllables, fast linking between words). The ear has to be retrained gradually. Start very slow and very simple, then increase speed and accent variety over the stages.

Graded listening sources (free)

Work down this list as the student improves. Do not jump to IELTS audio in Stage 1 — it will sound like noise and discourage them. Each level below names a type of free resource (so you can pick a current, working channel/site) plus what to listen for and how to use it in class.

Level 1 — Single words and very short phrases (absolute beginner)

  • Resource type: beginner ESL/EFL listening videos and apps that show a picture + say one word slowly (free "English for beginners" channels on YouTube; free starter lessons on apps like Duolingo or BBC Learning English's lowest-level clips). Children's first-words content (e.g. simple alphabet/number/animal videos) is also fine — it is slow and clearly enunciated.
  • Listen for: individual words the student already met in class; numbers, days, colours, common nouns.
  • How the teacher uses it: play one word, pause, ask the student to point to the right picture or write the word. Build confidence that they can catch sounds.

Level 2 — Slow, graded full sentences (early beginner)

  • Resource type: graded/slow-English listening channels (e.g. "Easy English" style slow-speech videos, BBC Learning English beginner series, VOA Learning English at its slowest "Let's Learn English" levels). These speak in full but slow, clear sentences with simple vocabulary.
  • Listen for: sentence shape — where one word ends and the next begins; question vs statement intonation; key content words.
  • How the teacher uses it: play a sentence, the student writes the words they recognise (not the whole sentence). Teacher fills the gaps and replays.

Level 3 — News in easy / simplified English (high beginner → low intermediate)

  • Resource type: "news in easy English" / "learning English" news (VOA Learning English news stories, BBC Learning English "News Review" at normal-but-clear speed, simplified-news podcasts for learners). Spoken clearly, slower than real news, real-world topics.
  • Listen for: the main idea, names, numbers, dates — the same information types IELTS asks about.
  • How the teacher uses it: play a short clip twice. After it, ask 2–3 simple comprehension points (What was it about? Any number? Any place name?). Student writes answers.

Level 4 — Podcasts and shows for English learners (intermediate)

  • Resource type: free learner podcasts (e.g. "Learn English" podcast series from British Council/BBC, ESL conversation podcasts). These use natural conversation but are pitched at learners and often come with free transcripts.
  • Listen for: natural connected speech, contractions ("gonna", "I'll", "don't"), turn-taking in a dialogue.
  • How the teacher uses it: listen once with no text, then a second time with the free transcript so the student sees how the sounds map to written words. This directly builds the ear for the IELTS conversation/dialogue sections.

Level 5 — Official IELTS practice listening (test preparation)

  • Resource type: the free official IELTS practice Listening tests and sample recordings published by the official IELTS bodies (the British Council / IDP / Cambridge "Take IELTS" practice materials). These use the real format and the real mix of accents.
  • Listen for: following along while reading the questions; catching spelled-out names and numbers; not panicking when the speaker is fast or has an unfamiliar accent.
  • How the teacher uses it: start by playing sections twice even though the real test plays once — build up to one play only as a later stage. Always do the answers together afterwards.

Level 6 — Real native media (stretch / real-world prep)

  • Resource type: free native-English public media (major public broadcasters' free news streams and podcasts) plus everyday native content the student enjoys (free news clips, simple documentaries with native speakers). This is real, unsimplified native-speaker speech.
  • Listen for: getting the gist even when not every word is clear — exactly what real life and the workplace demand.
  • How the teacher uses it: keep it low-pressure. The goal at this level is exposure and gist, not catching everything. Celebrate understanding the main point.

Weekly listening routine (for the student)

Keep it light and daily for a beginner — a little every day beats one long session. It grows across the stages.

Early stages (Stage 1–2): about 5 minutes a day, 5 days a week

  1. Listen to one short Level 1–2 clip (one word or one sentence at a time).
  2. Write down every word you recognise — just the words, don't worry about full sentences.
  3. Bring the list to class. The teacher reviews it, replays the clip, and fills in what was missed.

Middle stages: about 10–15 minutes a day

  • Mostly active listening (Level 3–4): listen, write what you caught, then check against the transcript.
  • Add a little passive listening: have English audio playing in the background while doing chores — just to get the ear used to the rhythm, no writing needed.

Later stages (IELTS-focused): about 20 minutes a day

  • One active session on Level 5 (official IELTS practice) with the question sheet.
  • Top up with Level 6 native media for real-accent exposure.

The simple loop at every stage: listen → write down the words you recognise → teacher reviews and replays. That loop is the whole method — easy to keep up, and it makes progress visible week to week.

Note on how this connects to lessons

Lessons from Stage 1 onward will point the student back to this resource as the IELTS listening "drip" — a small, steady habit running alongside the main course.

In class, listening practice usually takes the form of dictation: the teacher says a word, phrase, or sentence, and the student writes it down. This fits the core rule perfectly — the student is listening and writing, never speaking — and it is the most direct way to train the ear and check, word by word, what the student can and cannot yet hear.
