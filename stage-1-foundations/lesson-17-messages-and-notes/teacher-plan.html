Lesson 17 — Short Messages and Notes

How to teach this

This lesson is the student's first gentle taste of writing a short personal message, after several lessons of guided reading. Keep the reading texts very short (4–6 lines) and warm — the goal is recognition and confidence, not coverage.

This work leads directly toward IELTS General Training Writing Task 1, where the candidate writes a short letter (often informal) to invite, apologize, explain, request, or give information. The functions practised here (inviting, apologizing, asking a favour, leaving information) are exactly the building blocks of that task. You do not need to mention the word count or band scores yet — just plant the idea that "a friendly note has a beginning, a middle, and an ending." For the wider picture of the exam and where Writing Task 1 sits, see ielts/ielts-overview.md.

Remember the course rules: the student never speaks. Every task is reading, recognition, or writing. Never ask the student to say, repeat, or read anything aloud. Correct gently and in writing.

Objective

By the end of the lesson the student can:

  • read short personal messages and notes (texts, sticky notes, short informal emails) and understand their purpose;
  • write a short informal note of their own with a clear greeting → message → request/info → sign-off.

Target language

  • Informal openings: Hi…, Hey…, Hello…
  • Informal closings (sign-offs): See you, Thanks, Take care, Best, Bye for now
  • Common note functions:
    • Inviting — Do you want to…?, Come over…, Would you like to…?
    • Apologizing — Sorry, I can't…, Sorry about…
    • Asking a favour — Can you…?, Could you…?
    • Leaving information — I'll be late., Don't forget…, Let me know.

Flow with timings (~45–50 min)

Warm-up (5 min) Show the student two real-looking short notes (one text-message bubble, one sticky note on a fridge). Ask, in writing, "Which one is from a friend? Which one is for someone at home?" Let the student circle or write a short answer. No speaking — this is recognition only. Briefly explain that today we read short messages and then write one.

Present (10 min) On the board or sheet, lay out the three parts of every friendly note: greeting / message / sign-off. Show the openings (Hi, Hey) and closings (Thanks, See you, Take care, Best). Then introduce the four functions with one example each:

  • Invite — Do you want to come for dinner on Saturday?
  • Apologize — Sorry, I can't come tomorrow.
  • Ask a favour — Can you bring the keys?
  • Leave information — I'll be late. Don't forget the milk. Keep glosses light (e.g. favour = favor / um favor).

Practice (15 min) Work through Exercises (a)–(d) on the student sheet. Each begins with a solved example, so let the student read the example first, then continue alone in writing. Check answers together by pointing, not speaking-drills.

  • (a) Match 3 messages to purpose (invite / apologize / remind).
  • (b) Read one short informal email and answer 3–4 questions in writing.
  • (c) Fill the gaps in a note using the word bank of greetings/closings.
  • (d) Correct 3–4 clear errors.

Produce — student WRITES a note (10–12 min) Exercise (e): the student writes a short informal note (4–5 lines) replying to a given message, using the frame greeting → message → request/info → sign-off and the sentence starters provided. Sit beside them; if they stall, point to the frame, not the answer. This is the heart of the lesson.

Wrap-up (5 min) Read the student's note back silently together. Praise one strong thing (e.g. a clear sign-off). Note for yourself one pattern to revisit. Hand out homework.

Common mistakes & gentle corrections

  • Missing greeting or sign-off. A note feels unfinished without both. Gently: "A friendly note needs a hello and a goodbye — add Hi at the top and Thanks at the bottom."
  • Capital "I". The pronoun I is always a capital letter in English (unlike Portuguese eu). Circle the lowercase i and write the capital above it.
  • "Can you to bring…?" No to after can. Show: Can you bring…? — can + base verb.
  • Comma after the greeting. In English we usually write Hi Ana, with a comma, then start the message on the next line. Model it once.
  • Over-formal closings. If the student writes Yours sincerely on a note to a friend, gently swap it for Thanks or See you and explain that very formal closings are saved for official letters.

Homework

  1. Read the homework message on the student sheet and answer the two questions in writing.
  2. Write one short informal note (4–5 lines) inviting a friend to do something, using the frame greeting → message → request/info → sign-off.
  3. Underline your greeting and your sign-off so you can see all three parts are there.
