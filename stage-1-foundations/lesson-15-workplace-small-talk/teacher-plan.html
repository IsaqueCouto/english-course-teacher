Lesson 15 — Workplace Small-Talk and Politeness

How to teach this

This lesson builds the polite, everyday language your student will need on their first days at a new job abroad. Remember the two ground rules: the student does the spoken small-talk practice with you, live — you coach it conversationally — while the sheet is reading and writing only. Never ask the student to say, repeat, or read anything aloud. On the sheet, the student matches, rewrites, orders, corrects, and writes.

Keep the tone warm and encouraging. Read every example and email aloud yourself so the student hears natural rhythm, but the student's job on paper is recognition and writing. Use the light Portuguese glosses as a safety net when a phrase is new, then return to English quickly. Go gently: every exercise starts with a solved example, so always work through that example together before the student tries the rest.

The big idea to land: could and would make a request softer and more polite than can and will. At work, softer is safer.

Objective

The student can understand and write polite workplace phrases — greetings and small-talk openers, polite requests, and short messages or emails to a coworker or manager.

Target language

  • "How are you?" / "How's it going?" — Como vai? / Tudo bem?
  • "Did you have a good weekend?" — Teve um bom fim de semana?
  • "Could you…?" / "Would you mind…?" (polite requests) — Você poderia…? / Você se importaria de…?
  • "Can I help you?" — Posso ajudar?
  • "Excuse me" — Com licença / Desculpe
  • "I'm sorry, I'm late." — Desculpe, estou atrasado(a).
  • "Thank you." / "No problem." — Obrigado(a). / Sem problema.
  • Email basics: "Hi [name]," / "Thanks," / "Best," — Oi [nome], / Obrigado(a), / Atenciosamente,
  • Politeness note: could / would are softer than can / will.

Flow with timings (~45–50 min)

Warm-up (5–7 min) Greet the student warmly with "How are you?" and "Did you have a good weekend?" Coach a short spoken exchange live — this is your job, not the sheet's. Then point to the Key language box and read the phrases through together while the student follows along on the page.

Present (10 min) Teach the politeness idea at the heart of the lesson. Write two versions on paper:

  • "Give me the report." (blunt)
  • "Could you give me the report, please?" (polite) Ask the student to point to the polite one. Explain that could and would soften a request, and that please and thank you are normal and expected at work. Walk through the email-opener and sign-off phrases ("Hi [name]," / "Thanks," / "Best,") so they are familiar before the written practice.

Practice (15 min) Work through the sheet's exercises (a)–(e). Do each solved example together first, then let the student complete the rest in writing. Exercises cover: matching small-talk questions to answers, making blunt requests polite, ordering/filling a short message, correcting clear errors, and reading a manager's email to answer questions in writing.

Produce — student WRITES a short message (8–10 min) The student writes a short polite message (4–5 lines) to a coworker, using the frame on the sheet: greeting → reason → request → thanks → sign-off. Let them choose: asking for help, or saying they will be late. Offer the sentence starters as scaffolding. This is written work, not spoken.

Wrap-up (5 min) Read the student's finished message back to them and praise what worked. Highlight any place where could/would made it more polite. Preview the homework.

Common mistakes & gentle corrections

  • Using "can" where "could" is more polite. "Can you give me…" is fine, but at work "Could you…, please?" sounds warmer. Gently offer the softer version rather than marking it wrong.
  • Dropping "please." Portuguese speakers often rely on tone for politeness; in written English the word please carries it. Add it in.
  • Translating "Tudo bem?" literally. "All good?" is understandable but "How's it going?" or "How are you?" is more natural — show the natural phrase.
  • Forgetting the sign-off. A message that ends abruptly can feel cold. Remind them: "Thanks," or "Best," then their name.
  • "I'm sorry for late." The natural form is "I'm sorry, I'm late." or "Sorry I'm late." — correct gently and move on.

Homework

Set the homework printed on the student sheet: a short matching/correction review plus writing one more polite message. Tell the student to bring it to the next session so you can read it together.
