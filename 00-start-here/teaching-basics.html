Teaching Basics: Your Crash Course

You've never taught before. That's completely fine. This guide gives you everything you need to walk into a lesson and run it well. Read it once now, then come back to any section when you need it.

Your student is a Portuguese speaker who already has the basics — he reads and writes simple sentences. The course starts at Stage 1 (Foundations) and builds toward the IELTS General Training test (around November 2026). You'll meet 3-4 times a week. You speak both languages fluently, which is a huge advantage — but it's also the thing you'll have to hold back, as you'll see below.

Above all: be patient, be warm, and celebrate small wins. Confidence is fragile, so protect it while building real skill.

A note on speaking. The student materials (lesson sheets and homework) never require the student to speak, pronounce, or repeat — those tasks are recognition, reading, listening, and writing only. Speaking is something you coach live, in your own way, when the student is ready. So throughout this guide, whenever you see the student "repeating" or "answering," treat it as optional and pressure-free — wonderful if it happens naturally, never forced. In the lesson "Produce" stage, a beginner can show what they've learned by pointing, choosing, matching, or writing — not only by talking.

1. The Golden Rule: Don't Translate Everything

The single biggest mistake new teachers make is translating every word into Portuguese the moment the student looks confused. It feels helpful. It isn't. When you translate immediately, the student's brain learns "I don't need to work — the teacher will give me the Portuguese." That builds dependence on translation, not real recall.

Instead: elicit first. Make the student reach for the meaning before you hand it over. Point, gesture, mime, draw, show a picture, repeat the word, give the first sound. Let them guess. The little moment of effort where they think "hmm... apple?" is exactly where memory gets built. When the brain works to retrieve something, it remembers it far better than when it's spoon-fed.

The rule is simple: try to show or elicit before you translate. Translation is your last resort, not your first move.

Examples: Eliciting vs. Translating

Example A — teaching "apple"

  • Translating (avoid): "Apple means maçã." Done. The student nods and forgets it by next week.
  • Eliciting (do this): Hold up an apple (or a picture). Point to it. Say "Apple." Pause. Point again. "Apple?" Let them repeat. Take a bite, smile, say "I like apples." Only if they're truly lost do you confirm with a quick "sim, maçã" — and then go straight back to English: "Apple. Say it. Apple."

Example B — teaching "tired"

  • Translating (avoid): "Tired means cansado."
  • Eliciting (do this): Yawn loudly. Rub your eyes. Slump your shoulders. Say "Ohhh, I'm tired." Mime it again. Ask "Tired?" and watch their face light up when it clicks. They'll remember "tired" because they saw it.

Example C — teaching "between"

  • Translating (avoid): "Between means entre."
  • Eliciting (do this): Put a pen between two cups on the table. Point at the pen. "The pen is between the cups." Move it. "Now? Is it between?" (No.) Move it back. "Between." The position teaches the word.

If you've genuinely tried to elicit and the student is stuck or frustrated, a quick Portuguese word to unblock them is fine. The goal isn't to ban Portuguese — it's to make English the first thing you both reach for.

2. The Lesson Shape: PPP (Present → Practice → Produce)

Every lesson in this course follows the same three-part shape. Once you internalize it, planning and running a class becomes easy. PPP stands for Present, Practice, Produce.

  • Present = you show it. You introduce the new language. The student mostly listens and repeats. You do the heavy lifting here.
  • Practice = controlled / guided. The student uses the language, but inside guardrails you set. There's a "right answer." You're steering closely.
  • Produce = the student uses it freely. You step back. The student makes their own sentences, with their own real information. Mistakes are fine here — this is where it becomes theirs.

The energy shifts across the three: you talk most in Present, you share the floor in Practice, and the student talks most in Produce.

A concrete mini-example: teaching "I'm from ___"

Present (you show it). Point to yourself. "I'm from Brazil." Point to a map or just gesture. Say it again slowly: "I'm... from... Brazil." Write it on paper: I'm from _____. Say it a couple of times so they hear it clearly. The student just watches and listens, then repeats after you.

Practice (controlled/guided). Now the student says it, but you're holding the rails. Show them flashcards or names of cities: "Toronto." They say "I'm from Toronto." "São Paulo." "I'm from São Paulo." There's a clear right answer — you're just getting the structure into their mouth. You can also do a quick back-and-forth: you ask "Where are you from?" and they answer with the fixed pattern.

Produce (student uses it freely). Step back. Ask real questions and let them respond with true, personal answers: "Where are you from? Where is your family from? Where do you want to live?" Now they're combining "I'm from ___" with their own life. They might stumble. Good — that's real use, and it's the goal of the whole lesson.

If the student can't do the Produce stage, it usually means Practice needed more reps. That's a signal to slow down, not push harder.

3. Portuguese: When It's Allowed, When to Force English, How to Fade It

You both speak Portuguese, so the temptation to drift into it is constant. Use Portuguese as a precise tool, not a comfort blanket.

Portuguese is fine (especially early on) for:

  • Complex instructions the student couldn't yet follow in English: "Agora vamos praticar perguntas" before you've taught classroom language.
  • Abstract concepts and grammar logic that can't be mimed: explaining why we say "I am" not "I is," or what a syllable is.
  • Quickly checking meaning after you've tried to elicit: "Entendeu? Isso significa..." — a fast confirmation, then straight back to English.
  • Reassurance when the student is overwhelmed.

Force English (no Portuguese) for:

  • Greetings — "Hello / How are you? / Good, thanks" from day one.
  • Classroom phrases — once taught, these are always in English.
  • Anything you've already taught. If it came up last week, the student answers in English now. This is non-negotiable and it's how the language compounds.

How to fade Portuguese out over the weeks. Use Portuguese mainly for explaining a genuinely new grammar point; keep everything else in English. Then deliberately shrink even that over the weeks. Teach the student a few survival phrases early — "Can you repeat?", "What does ___ mean?", "I don't understand", "Slowly, please" — so he can ask for help in English instead of you switching to Portuguese.

Instead of "não entendeu?", say "Do you understand?" Instead of jumping to translate, prompt: "Ask me — 'What does it mean?'" By the time IELTS prep gets serious, aim for lessons that are almost entirely in English, with Portuguese reserved only for the occasional tricky concept.

4. Error Correction That Doesn't Crush Confidence

Beginners make constant errors. If you correct every single one, the student will be too scared to open their mouth. The art is choosing what to fix and fixing it kindly.

When to correct:

  • The error is in the exact thing you're teaching today. (Teaching past tense? Fix "I go yesterday.")
  • The error blocks understanding — you genuinely can't tell what they mean.
  • It's a repeated, fossilizing mistake you've heard several times.

When to let it go:

  • During a Produce / fluency moment when they're bravely stringing a real sentence together. Don't interrupt the courage. Note it and address it later.
  • When it's a tiny slip unrelated to today's target and the meaning is clear.
  • When they're already nervous and another correction would shut them down.

Three gentle correction techniques

1. Recasting (just say it back correctly). You don't label the mistake — you naturally repeat the correct version, and they absorb it.

Student: "Yesterday I go to the store." You: "Oh nice — you went to the store! What did you buy?"

The conversation keeps flowing, but they heard "went." Low pressure, very effective.

2. Self-correction prompts (let them fix it themselves). A small nudge so the student finds the error. This builds independence.

Student: "She have two cats." You: "She... ?" (pause, encouraging look) Student: "She... has two cats." You: "Yes!"

Or echo just up to the error with a rising, questioning tone: "She have?" — the lift signals "something's off here" without spelling it out.

3. Gentle direct correction (when a prompt doesn't land). If they can't find it, just give it cleanly and have them repeat once.

You: "We say 'she has.' Try it — 'She has two cats.'" Student: "She has two cats." You: "Perfect."

Always end on the correct version said by the student, and keep your face warm. Tone matters more than the words: a smile turns a correction into help, not a verdict.

5. Homework and Review

Little and often beats big and rare. The two habits below are what make lessons actually stick between sessions.

Giving homework (keep it small and doable). After 3-4 lessons a week, homework should be light — 5 to 15 minutes. The point is contact with the language between classes, not a burden. Good beginner homework:

  • "Write 5 sentences about your family using 'I have ___'." (e.g., "I have one sister.")
  • "Label 10 things in your kitchen with English sticky notes — fridge, cup, plate..."
  • "Write the days of the week 3 times, and circle today's day. I'll check it next class."
  • "Listen to this short song/clip and write down 3 words you recognize."

Make it concrete, tied to what you just taught, and something they can succeed at. Tell them clearly: you'll check it at the start of next class. (And then actually check it — otherwise they'll stop doing it.)

Starting each session with a 5-minute review. Begin every lesson with a quick, low-pressure warm-up that recycles last time's material. This wakes up the memory before you add anything new.

  • Quick-fire the previous vocabulary: hold up flashcards or point at objects, they say the word.
  • Ask 2-3 questions using last lesson's structure: "Where are you from? How are you today?"
  • Glance at the homework together — praise what's right, gently fix one thing.

Keep it fast and friendly, like a warm handshake with the language. If something from last time has clearly been forgotten, that's not failure — re-teach it briefly. Repetition over many short sessions is exactly how beginners learn.

6. How to Read a teacher-plan.md in This Course

Every lesson in this course comes with a teacher-plan.md. Read the whole plan before class, then just follow it. It's written so you don't have to invent anything — the thinking is already done. Here's what each section means:

  • "How to teach this" (the method note). A short note on the approach for this specific lesson — what to elicit, what to mime, where Portuguese is okay, what to watch for. Read this first; it frames everything else.
  • Objective + target words. What the student should be able to do by the end (e.g., "introduce themselves with 'I'm from ___'") and the exact vocabulary to teach. Don't add extra words — stay on target.
  • Flow with timings. The lesson minute-by-minute, in PPP order: warm-up → present → practice → produce → wrap-up. The timings are a guide, not a stopwatch — if a stage needs more reps, take them.
  • Portuguese bridge notes. Tells you exactly where Portuguese is allowed in this lesson and where to force English. Follow it; it's calibrated to fade Portuguese as the course goes on.
  • Common mistakes & gentle corrections. The errors this lesson tends to produce, with ready-made gentle corrections. Skim these before class so you're not caught off guard — you'll know the fix before the mistake even happens.
  • Homework. The small task to send the student home with. Give it at the end; check it at the start of next class.

That's the whole system. Read the plan, run the flow, elicit before you translate, correct kindly, end on a win. You're ready.
